About the Project

The Classical Indian Astronomy project aims to digitally reconstruct and simulate the behavior of ancient Indian observational instruments (Yantras) based on geographical coordinates. By utilizing modern web technologies and 3D rendering, this project brings historical astronomical methodologies to life. You can select any location within India, and the system reconstructs how key instruments would be built and oriented at that specific latitude and longitude.

Samrat Yantra

The Samrat Yantra is an enormous sundial consisting of a triangular gnomon with its hypotenuse parallel to the Earth's axis, flanked by a pair of quadrants on either side. It was used to measure time and the declination of celestial objects.

Mathematical Principle

The angle of the gnomon's hypotenuse exactly matches the latitude (φ) of the location. The height of the gnomon (H) relates to the base (B) through the tangent of the latitude:

tan(φ) = H / B

Nadi Valaya Yantra

The Nadi Valaya Yantra is an equatorial sundial representing the Earth's equator. It consists of two circular dials facing north and south, inclined to the horizon at an angle equal to the complement of the latitude.

Mathematical Principle

The principal dial surface is oriented parallel to the equatorial plane. Thus, the inclination (θ) from the local horizontal surface is exactly the observer's latitude's complement:

θ = (90° - φ)

Bhitti Yantra

The Dakshinottara Bhitti Yantra is a meridian dial consisting of a graduated vertical wall oriented exactly in the North-South plane. It is used to measure the meridian altitude and zenith distances of the Sun, Moon, and planets.

Mathematical Principle

The wall lies perfectly on the local meridian. A gnomon (peg) is inserted into the wall. As the sun transits the meridian, the shadow falls on the graduated arc on the wall. The zenith distance (z) can be calculated exactly by measuring the shadow:

tan(z) = shadow_length / gnomon_length
